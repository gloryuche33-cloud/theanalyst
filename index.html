Brew Connect: one connected system for a UK brewing group

A brewery was running on manual records, outdated spreadsheets and guesswork. We investigated why production slipped, and proposed an integrated Digital Brewery Management System linking Sales, Brewing, Quality Control, Bottling, Inventory and Maintenance.

My role
Business Analyst within a project team
Client
UK brewing group (name withheld)
Type
Business analysis engagement: current-state review and solution proposal
Methods
Fishbone, SWOT, gap analysis, As-Is and To-Be mapping, BRD
Status
Recommendations and BRD delivered; solution proposed, not built

The problem

Important brewery activities relied on manual processes, ageing equipment and weak communication between departments. Demand forecasts were driven by intuition rather than data. Recipes, fermentation records and inventory were tracked by hand.

The knock-on effects were equipment breakdowns, bottling-line delays, inconsistent quality, heavier workloads for staff and a real risk of stock shortages and unhappy customers.

My contribution

  • Analysed the existing processes and identified the gaps between how the brewery worked and how it needed to work.
  • Assessed the root causes behind manual record-keeping, production delays, inaccurate forecasting and poor communication between departments.
  • Supported the process mapping, the SWOT analysis, the business requirements and the final recommendations.
  • Considered stakeholder needs and how shared information could improve inventory management, production planning and decision-making.
  • Worked with the project team to develop and present recommendations.

I worked as part of a project team. The deliverables below are shared team outputs that I contributed to.

Approach

  • Investigate: documented the current operation and its pain points.
  • Diagnose: used a Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram to group root causes across production, equipment and communication.
  • Position: ran a SWOT analysis of the organisation’s strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats.
  • Compare: carried out a gap analysis of the current state against the desired future state.
  • Design: mapped the As-Is process, then a To-Be swimlane showing how six departments would work together.
  • Specify and cost: wrote the Business Requirements Document and an illustrative cost view covering software, integration, equipment sensors, training and implementation.

What I delivered

  • Fishbone diagram of the operational root causes.
  • SWOT analysis of the organisation’s business position.
  • Gap analysis comparing current operations with the desired future state.
  • As-Is process map of existing brewery operations.
  • To-Be swimlane diagram showing the improved cross-department workflow.
  • Business Requirements Document defining the problem, the Brew Connect solution, key requirements and expected outcomes.
  • Illustrative financial analysis of implementation costs.

What we found

  • Manual record-keeping and outdated spreadsheets created information gaps.
  • Poor communication between Sales and Production affected planning and customer commitments.
  • Breakdowns and reactive maintenance contributed to production delays.
  • Inconsistent quality monitoring created a risk of discarded batches.
  • Repetitive administrative tasks added pressure on employees.

Expected improvements

  • More accurate demand forecasting and inventory management.
  • Fewer production delays and less equipment downtime.
  • Better product consistency and quality control.
  • Better communication between departments.
  • Faster, data-driven management decisions.
  • Less admin workload and better customer service.

These are expected benefits from the proposed design. The solution was a recommendation and was not implemented, so no measured results are claimed.

What I took from it

This project taught me to start from the business problem rather than the technology: investigate the operation, find root causes, compare current and future states, then document requirements that a delivery team can act on. It also sharpened how I work in a team and present recommendations to stakeholders.

Card Dispute Management System for a UK retail bank

Customers raising a card dispute were waiting an average of 35 days, with no visibility once a case was open. I helped shape a digital journey that makes disputes faster, trackable and auditable while keeping fraud and compliance controls intact.

My role
Business Analyst: dispute intake and workflow, evidence and decision framework, operational controls
Client
UK retail bank (name withheld)
Type
Business analysis engagement: process redesign and delivery planning
Tools
Jira (project key CDMS), Draw.io
Status
Analysis package and sprint-ready backlog; no build or pilot

The problem

Card disputes ran through a shared spreadsheet. Intake arrived by chat and phone, evidence by chat or email, and the customer had no view of progress after raising a case. That led to repeat contacts, complaints and seasonal backlogs for a Customer Operations team of roughly 120 people.

Fraud cases sat in the same queue as ordinary disputes, which delayed protective action.

Goals

  • Cut average resolution time by streamlining intake, evidence collection and the decision workflow.
  • Let customers submit and track disputes themselves for eligible transactions within 120 days.
  • Collect structured evidence and apply configurable dispute policies for consistency and auditability.
  • Freeze a card immediately on suspected fraud and prioritise fraud cases.
  • Centralise intake, evidence, chargebacks and outcomes, and notifications in one place.
  • Connect to Core Banking so eligible customers get a temporary credit within two working days.
  • Support controls for reconciliation, complaint handling and monitoring of unusual dispute patterns.

My contribution

  • Intake and workflow: led the redesign of dispute intake and the end-to-end flow, turning the manual process into a clear journey from submission through evidence to decision, aimed at removing delay.
  • Evidence and decisions: defined the evidence and decision framework so requirements were consistent, cases could be assessed more reliably and decisions made faster with a clear audit trail.
  • Operational controls: supported coordination across case ownership, status visibility, exception handling, fraud prioritisation and reconciliation.

Approach

  • Analysis: problem statements, current-state pain points, objectives, a business case covering benefits, volumes, costs and risks.
  • Stakeholders: stakeholder identification, a Power/Interest matrix and a RACI matrix.
  • Process mapping: As-Is and To-Be swimlane diagrams in Draw.io, moving from manual intake to a digital self-service journey with fraud prioritisation, evidence, chargebacks, credits, notifications, reconciliation and complaints.
  • Requirements: a list of 15 main system features, with gap analysis against the current process.
  • Agile delivery: 13 user stories in the standard “As a…, I want… so that…” format with acceptance criteria, organised in a Jira backlog (project CDMS) across three sprints, with proposed story-point estimation.
  • Risk: sensitive evidence, incorrect credits, integration failure, fraud prioritisation and seasonal demand, each with a practical mitigation.
  • Success measures: resolution time, first response, temporary-credit timeliness, complaints, fraud response and reconciliation accuracy.

What I found

  • Fraud cases share a queue with ordinary disputes, which delays protective action.
  • Customers have no visibility once a case is raised, which drives repeat contacts and complaints.
  • Today’s 35 days break down as 5 for first response, 10 for evidence, 12 for the external response and 8 for the final decision and credits.
  • The business case used an indicative 108,000 disputes a year. Saving 15 minutes per case would free about 27,000 staff hours. This is an illustration, not a measured result.

Estimates, volumes and savings in this case study are indicative. The RACI and story points still need validation with the project sponsor and operational teams.

Where it stands

The Jira backlog holds 13 stories across three sprints, all currently in To Do with no estimates entered. Acceptance criteria exist for the main customer and operations journeys. Two gaps remain: a policy-manager configuration story and an analyst closure story.

The next practical step is to lock the remaining acceptance criteria, estimates and sprint dates before any development or pilot. Overall, the work sets out a realistic path from a spreadsheet-driven process to a controlled digital journey, with fraud and compliance controls kept in place.
